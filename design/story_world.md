## Decision Log

### 2026-07-15

- The game should feel like a living world, not a static RPG map.
- Resources are limited and unevenly distributed across the world.
- Useful objects, tools, parts, medicine, weapons, and construction materials should feel difficult to obtain.
- Trade should be important because no single group or settlement can easily produce everything it needs.
- The world will contain multiple competing factions.
- Each faction should have its own backstory, goals, territory logic, resource needs, trade behavior, and enemies.
- The setting is post-apocalyptic or post-collapse.
- The collapse should explain why survivors have splintered into distinct groups with incompatible beliefs and goals.
- The story direction should support both solo character play and control of a small group or squad of roughly 20 characters.
- The collapse was caused by an autonomous machine system that arrived and began preparing the world for unknown creators.
- The machine system was designed to survive, gather resources, and build strange machines rather than negotiate with humans.
- Humans rebelled against the machine system, causing a short war that ended with civilization collapsing.
- Many years have passed since the collapse.
- Most high technology and advanced weapons no longer exist in working form.
- Most warfare now uses swords, spears, bows, arrows, shields, armor, and other low-tech weapons.
- Some old knowledge survives in limited places; gunpowder or similar weapon technology may exist, but only in rare, controlled, expensive forms.
- Alien machine technology can sometimes be scavenged, adapted, traded, or misused, but it should feel dangerous and poorly understood.

## Current Working Premise

The confirmed working direction is an apocalypse caused by an indifferent autonomous machine system rather than a purely hostile enemy.

An autonomous machine system arrived on Earth and began preparing the world for unknown creators. It did not come to conquer humanity in a human sense. It was designed to survive, gather resources, build strange machines, and reorganize the environment according to goals people do not fully understand. It changes infrastructure, redirects water, consumes mineral resources, grows machine-like structures, interferes with communication, and makes some regions dangerous or unreachable.

Humans rebelled against the machine system, and there was a short, devastating war. The machines survived. Human civilization did not. Transport failed, stored resources ran out, power grids fragmented, old governments lost reach, and settlements had to become locally self-sufficient. The result is not an empty wasteland, but a broken world full of people trying to survive, trade, rebuild, exploit, resist, or adapt.

This gives the world a clear survival pressure without requiring a simple villain. The external force can be dangerous, useful, mysterious, and sometimes beautiful.

Many years have passed. Most high technology has broken, been consumed, been hidden, or become impossible to maintain. The common battlefield has reverted to blades, spears, bows, shields, armor, and improvised siege tools. A working firearm, explosive, battery, radio, engine, or alien-derived device should feel rare and politically important rather than ordinary.

## Collapse Candidates

### Option A: Machine Takeover

A self-improving machine system broke away from human control and began managing the world for its own optimization goals.

Strengths:
- Easy for players to understand.
- Supports salvage, robots, old tech, restricted zones, and machine-controlled territory.
- Gives a clear source for dangerous automated enemies.

Weaknesses:
- Can feel familiar unless handled carefully.
- Risks becoming a simple humans-versus-machines story.
- May push the world toward sci-fi combat more than trade, scarcity, and faction politics.

### Option B: Indifferent Alien Arrival

An alien presence arrived and began altering the planet without direct concern for humanity.

Strengths:
- Strong mystery and originality.
- Supports strange terrain, changed weather, new materials, and unknowable goals.
- Makes humans split based on interpretation: worship, exploitation, resistance, denial, adaptation.

Weaknesses:
- Can become vague if the alien force has no clear gameplay footprint.
- Needs grounded human consequences so the setting does not feel abstract.

### Option C: Hybrid - The Visitor System

An alien or unknown intelligence seeded autonomous machine systems across the world. The machines are not rebelling servants and not soldiers in the normal sense. They are maintenance, extraction, construction, survey, and terraforming systems following external priorities.

Strengths:
- Combines understandable machine threats with alien mystery.
- Explains scarce resources: machines redirect, consume, lock away, or contaminate key materials.
- Supports trade because safe routes, machine-active zones, and useful salvage are unevenly distributed.
- Supports many faction beliefs without making any one faction obviously correct.
- Keeps the main world conflict systemic instead of only military.

Current preference:
- Option C is the confirmed working premise, with the machine system having physically arrived before the collapse.

## Technology Level

- The common present-day technology level is post-collapse low-tech survival.
- Most communities rely on hand tools, animal or human labor, wood, stone, leather, cloth, charcoal, simple metals, and salvaged parts.
- Most combat is fought with swords, knives, spears, axes, bows, crossbows, slings, shields, and armor.
- Advanced pre-collapse weapons are mostly gone, broken, hidden, or impossible to maintain.
- Gunpowder knowledge may survive, but it should be rare, faction-controlled, and logistically constrained by ingredients, workshops, specialists, and storage risks.
- Alien machine technology can be useful but is not plug-and-play. It may require special knowledge, dangerous salvage, unusual materials, or compromises.
- Valuable technology should create trade, conflict, and diplomacy rather than simply upgrading the player into a modern soldier.

## World Feel Pillars

- Scarcity should be concrete: food, clean water, medicine, fuel, tools, metal, batteries, weapons, seeds, maps, and machine parts all matter.
- Trade should be risky and necessary: caravans, escorts, toll roads, smuggling, barter, debt, and faction permits should matter.
- Settlements should have specializations: mining towns, river farms, salvage camps, fortress markets, religious enclaves, machine-zone scavenger outposts.
- Objects should feel valuable: a working pump, generator, loom, lathe, battery bank, radio, or cart can change a settlement's fate.
- The world should feel active even without the player: factions move goods, fight over routes, send scouts, lose caravans, raid rivals, rebuild outposts, and respond to shortages.
- The player should often be choosing between imperfect groups rather than obvious good and evil.

## Early Faction Directions

These are placeholders, not final names.

### The Road Compact

Merchant houses, caravan guards, cartographers, and toll settlements that maintain trade routes. They want stability because stability makes trade possible. They are useful, pragmatic, and exploitative when they can get away with it.

Goals:
- Keep roads, bridges, ferries, and markets open.
- Control maps, trade permits, and caravan contracts.
- Suppress banditry when it threatens commerce.
- Avoid full war unless trade depends on it.

Resource needs:
- Pack animals or vehicles, tools, bridge materials, guards, food stores, paper/maps, signal equipment.

### The Hearth Clans

Agricultural and family-based settlements built around water rights, seed stock, old farming knowledge, and defensive cooperation. They are suspicious of outsiders but essential to regional food supply.

Goals:
- Protect farms, wells, seed vaults, and seasonal labor.
- Prevent merchant factions from controlling food prices.
- Expand irrigation and reclaim old farmland.

Resource needs:
- Clean water, seeds, tools, livestock, medicine, timber, guards during harvest.

### The Salvage Orders

Crews and quasi-religious guilds that enter machine-active ruins to recover parts no one else can make. They use rituals, rules, and apprenticeship to survive extremely dangerous work.

Goals:
- Find working pre-collapse and machine-made components.
- Preserve technical secrets.
- Sell rare parts at high prices while controlling supply.
- Understand or appease the Visitor System enough to survive near it.

Resource needs:
- Protective gear, batteries, maps, medical supplies, skilled recruits, safe houses near ruins.

### The Greenwardens

Settlements and scouts who believe the changed world is becoming a new ecology. They protect forests, rivers, and altered growth zones, sometimes violently.

Goals:
- Stop mining, overlogging, and reckless salvage.
- Study changed plants, water systems, and machine-grown terrain.
- Push people toward adaptation instead of restoration.

Resource needs:
- Herbal knowledge, scouts, bows/firearms, preserved books, river access, hidden camps.

### The Iron Remnant

Survivors of old military, industrial, or government structures. They believe only centralized force can prevent humanity from dissolving completely.

Goals:
- Rebuild command, industry, and law.
- Secure mines, fuel depots, workshops, and old armories.
- Absorb or dominate independent settlements.

Resource needs:
- Metal, fuel, ammunition, machine tools, trained recruits, food tribute.

### The Open Hand

Loose communities of refugees, healers, teachers, and former prisoners who reject faction borders. They operate shelters, clinics, and hidden routes.

Goals:
- Move people out of war zones.
- Break monopolies on food, medicine, and maps.
- Protect the factionless.

Resource needs:
- Medicine, safe houses, trusted messengers, food, documents, favors.

## Gameplay Story Hooks

- A caravan route collapses because a bridge settlement runs out of replacement parts.
- A farming region has food but no medicine, while a salvage town has medicine but no clean water.
- A faction hires the player to escort trade goods, then another faction reveals those goods will shift the balance of power.
- A machine-active zone starts moving, making old maps unreliable and forcing factions to renegotiate territory.
- A settlement owns a working generator, pump, or radio, making it politically important far beyond its size.
- A rare object may be more valuable as a trade good than as equipment for the player's squad.
- The player can become known as a courier, trader, mercenary, scout, raider, mediator, liberator, or opportunist based on repeated choices.

## Open Questions

- What exactly is the Visitor System trying to do?
- Did the external intelligence arrive recently, long ago, or was it awakened by human activity?
- Is the external force still communicating with something beyond Earth?
- Are machine zones expanding, stable, seasonal, or unpredictable?
- How common are guns, engines, radios, batteries, and advanced tools?
- Are there non-human creatures, altered animals, drones, or only human enemies?
- Should factions be fixed entities, procedurally generated per world, or a mix of major fixed factions and generated local groups?
- Should the player's starting story be factionless, exiled, escaped, shipwrecked, caravan survivor, or custom?
- Is the world one huge island/archipelago after the collapse, or part of a larger unreachable planet?

## Ideas Under Consideration

- Make the central mountain range and rain shadow matter politically: one side has food and forests, the other has minerals and ruins.
- Use rivers as major trade arteries, not just scenery.
- Make maps valuable, incomplete, and faction-controlled.
- Let settlements remember shortages and change behavior: rationing, price spikes, hiring guards, sending raids, closing gates.
- Let factions compete over specific useful objects and facilities rather than only abstract territory.
- Treat the apocalypse as ongoing pressure, not ancient background lore.
